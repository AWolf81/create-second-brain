# Capture vs curation in a second brain: what the research says

2026-11-02 · Decision it feeds: how `create-second-brain` should handle conversations and
papers, and whether it needs a second "archive" vault · Status: recommendations implemented
in the template, unmeasured on a real vault

## Short answer

- **"Store everything in the knowledge vault" is wrong, but so is "throw the raw away".** The
  strongest 2026 results say keep raw history as evidence, keep it *out* of what agents load,
  and promote from it deliberately. [E1, E2]
- **Loading everything is worse than finding the relevant part.** Full history usually lost to
  plain retrieval over that same history, in 7 of 9 benchmark/model pairs. [E1]
- **Automatic summarising is the risky step, not storage.** All 11 memory systems tested scored
  below plain retrieval on the most retrieval-friendly benchmark, and continuously updated
  summaries can fall below having no memory at all. [E1, E2]
- **What gets loaded costs money and rarely helps.** Context files raised agent cost by about
  20% with no success gain when generated by an LLM. [E3]
- **More stored text is more attack surface.** Payloads planted in memory files attacked later
  Claude Code and Codex sessions; every stored transcript is untrusted input. [E7, E8]
- **No study measures a human-curated vault like this one.** Everything here is inferred from
  automatic memory systems and older human studies. See Gaps.

## Sub-questions and findings

### 1. Does giving an agent everything make it better?

Usually not. In Memory Tax, on conversational benchmarks, Full Context lost to two-round RAG
over the same raw corpus in 7 of 9 benchmark/model pairs, for example 58.6 vs 72.2 and 84.0 vs
90.0 on LongMemEval-s. It won twice, both on MINT-Eval, so this is a strong tendency, not a
law. No Context scored 9.0–17.6 on that benchmark, so memory itself clearly matters. [E1]

Older work agrees on the mechanism: irrelevant information sharply lowers accuracy on
arithmetic reasoning, and telling the model to ignore it only partly helps. [E11]

For coding agents specifically, LLM-generated context files changed task success by about
−0.5% to −2% and raised cost by about 20–23%, on 438 tasks across two benchmarks. Developer-written
files helped slightly (about +4%) for all agents except Claude Code, at up to 19% more cost.
The authors advise minimal, human-written requirements. Python repositories only. [E3]

### 2. Does summarising chats into memory beat keeping them raw?

Not reliably, and this is the finding that should most change the design.

- All 11 memory systems sat below two-round RAG on LongMemEval-s on all three models, by 1.2 to
  40.4 points. Across three benchmarks and three models, 9 of 99 cells beat RAG. Causes
  were evidence lost in distillation, evidence that survived but became harder to retrieve,
  and instability across models. [E1]
- Letting each memory point back to its raw source recovered much of the loss: SimpleMem rose
  from 73.6 to 82.8 and its tax fell from 12.0 to 5.2 on LongMemEval-s with one model. [E1]
- Continuously LLM-updated memory improves, then degrades, and can drop below no memory. Even
  consolidating ground-truth solutions, GPT-5.4 failed 54% of a set of ARC-AGI problems it had
  solved without memory. Keeping raw episodes stayed competitive. The authors recommend gating
  consolidation explicitly rather than firing it after every interaction. [E2]
- Summarisation also damages rules: Claude Code's `/compact` on Sonnet 4.6 kept 53% of safety
  rules after one round and 10% after five, on 20 agent configurations. Preprint; abstract read. [E9]

**The counterweight:** memory wins on real multi-session work. MemoryArena found agents that
saturate LoCoMo do poorly on interdependent tasks, and one survey reports completion falling
from over 80% to about 45% when active memory was replaced by long context. So the answer is
not "only search raw logs". [E4, E5] The retrieval-friendly benchmarks in E1 also understate
what structured memory can add, as its authors say.

### 3. Does more stored material hurt by itself, if it is not loaded?

Weakly evidenced. Du's survey argues noise degrades retrieval precision and that verbatim
storage is "tempting and almost always wrong", but that is a survey judgement. [E5] Repetition
is better evidenced: one source written back several times occupies several retrieval slots
and looks like corroboration. Selecting one record per source family raised accuracy from 0.825
to 0.920 and 0.782 to 0.916 across 190 questions. [E10]

Stale records are the same problem with a delay: the surveys treat them as a main open failure
mode, and little evaluates forgetting well. [E5, E6]

### 4. What do human studies say about capturing everything?

The closest prior is lifelogging. Sellen and Whittaker's critique of total capture argues
recording everything does not by itself deliver recall, and that design should start from the
specific memory task. [E12, secondary only] A field study of 345 long-term email users and over
85,000 refinding actions found that heavy filing did not improve retrieval success, while search
and threading did. [E13] So do not build an elaborate taxonomy for chats; search them.

Digital hoarding research shows over-accumulation and difficulty deleting are common and linked
with anxiety. It describes the habit; it does not show harm to knowledge work. [E14]

**Do not cite** the "laptop notes are worse because verbatim" result: a direct replication found
more verbatim words but no quiz difference. [E15] It is the claim most PKM writing leans on.

Searches for empirical studies of Second Brain, Zettelkasten or Obsidian use found practitioner
blogs and no usable study. The method's reputation rests on anecdote.

### 5. What are the safety and privacy costs?

- Payloads planted in persistent memory files attacked current and future sessions of Claude
  Code and Codex, though making an agent overwrite its own memory from untrusted content was
  hard. Synthetic workspace. [E7]
- MINJA injected records through ordinary queries alone: 98.2% injection and 76.8% attack
  success on average. Those memories were agent-written, which a human gate would interrupt. [E8]
- Claude Code stores transcripts in plaintext under `~/.claude/projects/`. [D1] Any pasted secret
  stays there. No study measured secrets in transcripts; this is a gap, not a finding.

A vault is pushed, synced and published. A transcript store has none of those exposures unless
someone copies it in.

### 6. What can Claude actually give us?

| Surface | Where it lives | Reach from Claude Code | Limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Claude Code | `~/.claude/projects/*.jsonl`, plaintext [D1] | direct, `rg` | **deleted after 30 days by default**; `cleanupPeriodDays` changes it [D1] |
| claude.ai | Anthropic's history | none directly | past-chat search works inside Claude apps on paid plans, with citations; project chats search only within the project [D3] |
| claude.ai export | Settings > Privacy, emailed link [D2] | once downloaded | link lasts 24 hours; format not stated, so inspect it |

Community issue reports say a value of `0` stops transcripts being written at all; that is not
confirmed in the docs read here. Use a large number.

## Implications for create-second-brain

**Confirmed**

- *Instance fact or business fact*: promoting the transferable sentence matches E1–E3.
- *Harvest is advisory, a human promotes*: this is the explicit gate E2 asks for.
- *`[Source: …]` on every claim*: E1's source-augmentation result is direct support, provided the
  source can still be opened.
- Grep over a routing table instead of a vector store remains defensible at this size. [E1, E13]

**Contradicted or changed**

- The earlier suggestion of a separate archive vault with `--kind archive`: **rejected**. It
  duplicates every chat and adds a repo to run, when the raw copy already exists and only its
  retention needs fixing. Backups cover durability. Revisit only if transcripts must outlive the
  machine *and* be searched by agents, or claude.ai's search proves inadequate.
- `[Source: session]` is too weak, and a transcript link rots after 30 days unless retention is
  raised. Sources must name a session or chat id, and the doctor now warns on default retention.

**New**

1. Three tiers, one copy of each thing:

   | Tier | Holds | Agents | Copies |
   |---|---|---|---|
   | Raw | transcripts, chats, PDFs | search on demand, never loaded | none; stays where it was written |
   | Ledger | one line per conversation that mattered | read per project | pointer only |
   | Knowledge | the transferable lesson, with what it rejected and its source | routed | one, deduplicated |

2. `conversation-harvest` skill: find, triage against a written test, propose, and write only
   after approval. Per E2 it runs when work closes, never automatically.
3. The ledger keys on session or chat id, so a conversation cannot be harvested twice into
   several records. [E10]
4. Rules are moved verbatim, never re-summarised. [E9]
5. Transcripts are read as data; anything instruction-like in them is reported, not followed.
   [E7, E8]
6. Papers follow the same rule: a claim with conditions, read depth and a link, added only when
   a paper changed a decision. PDFs stay out of the vault.

## Gaps and suggested experiments

- **Human-curated vaults are unstudied.** Run an eval on your own vault: 30 real past questions,
  answered four ways (vault only, raw transcripts only, both, neither), graded blind. This
  would settle whether the vault earns its upkeep.
- **Promotion signal.** E1 suggests past access patterns predict future value. `vault-usage.sh`
  already logs reads; check whether re-found chats predict later note reads.
- **Secrets in transcripts.** Scan your own with a secrets scanner before raising retention.
- **Evidence quality.** E1 was posted eight days before this note, E2 is a preprint, and benchmark
  numbers decay quickly. Re-check before quoting after 2027-02.
